Hormozi on consulting

Russell Raath on Alex Hormozi’s service-business advice. Steal the commercial clarity. Do not copy the agency tactics.

Source: Russell Raath, Oct 4, 2026, 16 min · YouTube

The one line

Consulting is not a high-ticket service. It is a trust and risk-transfer business. Speed, price, offer, and demand only stick after the client trusts your judgment.

The test

Does the advice bring you closer to a client’s live business problem — or does it only help you look more like a consultant?

Steal

  • Fix the offer before marketing, sales, or price.
  • Name a live decision, not a category.
  • Know real delivery cost. Stop pricing from an old salary.
  • High-touch only for clients who can act.
  • Present the fee live. Never hide behind the PDF.
  • Give away the thinking. Buyers hire judgment, not secrets.
  • Take action — but pick work that teaches the market you want.

Don’t copy

  • “Do it in 24 hours” as a consulting model.
  • Price = cost × 5 or 10.
  • The middle: not cheap/automated, not high-touch with stakes.
  • Outcome guarantees you don’t control.
  • Vague “strategy / transformation / leadership” offers.
  • Hiding useful thinking, or posting fake expertise.
  • Volume outreach that burns trust. That’s reputational debt.

1. Make the offer a reason to buy

Consultants misdiagnose a marketing problem when they have an offer problem. “Strategy transformation” is a category, not a reason to buy.

A serious offer names the problem or decision, the movement you can credibly create, and enough concrete work that someone can defend hiring you internally.

Weak: “12-week strategic transformation package.” Stronger: in 6 weeks, help the executive team decide whether the performance problem is strategy, operating, or leadership alignment — and leave with decisions, priorities, and next steps.

Raath’s “authority offer”: accumulated judgment around a consequential moment. Not manufactured certainty. Before you buy traffic, ask whether a serious buyer can tell when to call you.

2. Charge more — but be honest about value

Hormozi: cost the delivery, then 5–10×. Useful discipline: know whether the work can support prep, senior attention, follow-through, and a healthy business.

Diverge on the multiple. Split value three ways:

  • Potential — total upside if the problem is solved.
  • Attributable — the portion your work can credibly influence.
  • Realizable — what the client is likely to capture after leadership, politics, capital, tech, and the market have their say.

A $3B → $6B growth brief does not make your work worth $3B. Charge enough to do the work at the level the problem deserves, and enough that the client treats the decision seriously. Premium price does not create value. It can create the conditions where value is more likely to be realized.

3. Pick an end of the market

Hormozi’s blunt split: go upmarket (high-touch, proven model, real metrics) or downmarket (cheap, almost no delivery, CAC math). The middle is where people die.

If you sell high-touch judgment, choose a client with a problem that justifies it — proven model, meaningful stakes, decision-making authority, ability to act. Not a client who expects you to invent the strategy, repair sales, motivate the team, and guarantee revenue.

Niche is not a LinkedIn label. It is the market’s explanation for why it should trust you with a specific problem. You can standardize the method. You cannot automate away the judgment they are paying for.

4. Don’t hide behind the proposal

Hormozi: never present an invoice by email. Discuss it live so you can answer the real objection.

Agree. A proposal cannot hear hesitation. It cannot tell if the buyer is scared of the fee, internal politics, implementation, or their own credibility if it fails.

On guarantees: risk reversal helps, but do not guarantee an outcome you don’t control. Stand behind prep, process, and what you personally deliver. Reduce risk with proof, bounded scope, transparent responsibilities, and a process the buyer can defend internally.

5. Give away the thinking

Two consulting-marketing mistakes: hide everything useful, or publish vague thought leadership that helps nobody decide.

A serious buyer does not hire you because the information is scarce. They hire you because applying it to a politically complicated situation requires judgment. Show how you tell a strategy problem from an operating problem. Show the questions that expose false alignment.

Dislike of bad LinkedIn content is not an excuse to skip marketing. Measure meaningful trust-building, not messages sent. Volume that damages trust is not demand generation.

6. Act — but vote for the practice you want

Waiting for the perfect offer is avoidance. Action without selection traps you in the wrong market, at the wrong fee, known for work you don’t want more of.

Ask: will this project pay, and what will it make possible — proof, a sponsor, a method, buyer language? A cash-flow project can be rational. Don’t pretend every paid project is building the practice you want. Your first project is a vote.

What they’re buying

Not mystery. Precision. Access to judgment earned in situations that resemble theirs. A process that helps them make a hard decision. Confidence the work can survive contact with their organization.

Should consultants listen to Hormozi? Yes. Copy the commercial discipline. Don’t copy the tactics without understanding what the buyer is risking.
